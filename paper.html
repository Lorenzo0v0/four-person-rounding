Five dishes or six?
Exact horizons for online rounding
among four people

ABSTRACT

When four people split a succession of shared items, who should pay the cents left over on each item? We compute the smallest worst-case cumulative rounding error at every horizon. If each item has one extra cent, an adaptive adversary needs six items to force the limiting error of 7/6 cents. If an item may have several extra cents, five suffice. In both games the same greedy rule is minimax at every horizon. Its 195 reachable discrepancy vectors, up to relabeling, coincide across the games even though their shortest paths differ.

1. Two rounding games

Four accounts start at zero. An item costs an integer number of cents and is shared equally by a set S of k people. Write its cost as kq + r with 0 ≤ r < k. Charge each person q cents and select exactly r distinct participants to pay one additional cent each. Each item is settled irrevocably before the next arrives. Exact divisions have no effect and may be omitted.

In the single game r = 1. The batch game permits every 1 ≤ r < k. Write di for actual cumulative payment minus the sum of person i’s exact fractional shares. The loss through item h is the largest |di| experienced by any person at any prefix through h. The value V(h) is the smallest worst-case loss an online policy can guarantee. The adversary may observe every prior allocation before choosing the next item. All bounds are pathwise.

Greedy rule. Among the current participants, charge the extra cents to the r people with the lowest current discrepancies. Ties may be broken arbitrarily.

2. How to force the last twelfth

3. Which states can greedy reach?

Theorem 2. From the zero vector, the two games have the same set of 195 sorted reachable greedy states. The maximum over this set of any absolute coordinate is 14 units. The largest shortest-path distance from zero is 11 items in the single game and 9 in the batch game.

Sign symmetry. In a batch round with group size k and remainder r, negate every starting discrepancy and replace r by k − r. The complement of the original payers is a valid greedy choice, and all increments negate. Applying this to each round shows that batch reachability is closed under negation for any population size. For four people, equality of the two reachable sets transfers this symmetry to the single game. This verifies the four-person case of the attainable-score question in [1, §2].

4. Finite verification

Greedy orbit. Store the four integer discrepancies as a sorted tuple x. Permuting labels permutes both legal requests and legal replies, and exchanging two tied payers gives the same sorted successor. Hence sorting preserves reachable orbits and the value of the adversarial game. For each subset of positions and each allowed remainder, increment its r least coordinates as described on page 2 and sort again. Breadth-first search from zero runs until the queue is empty. It explores 11 requests per state in the single game and 17 in the batch game.

The certificate lists each tuple and its two distances. An independent rational-arithmetic checker enumerates all greedy tie choices. For each game it checks closure, a predecessor one level nearer the origin for each nonzero state, and the inequality dist(y) ≤ dist(x) + 1 on every edge x → y. These conditions certify exact reachability and distances in Theorem 2.

Minimax. Fix an integer bound b. Let W(x, h) mean the allocator can keep every prefix inside the bound for h more items. A state outside the bound loses immediately. Inside the bound, W(x, 0) is true. For h ≥ 1 the recursion is

for every request (S, r), some T ⊆ S of size r satisfies W(next(x, S, r, T), h − 1).

Induction on h gives the equivalence to online feasibility. Scanning b from 0 to 14 gives Table 1 for h ≤ 6. Taking the largest coordinate magnitude up to each depth of the greedy orbit gives the same entries. Beyond six, prefix loss is nondecreasing and the closed orbit supplies the matching upper bound. This completes the computer-assisted proof of Theorem 1.

Relation to prior work

Fagin and Williams [1, p. 136] explicitly report f(4) = 7/6 for the largest positive score of single-driver greedy. Ajtai et al. [2] analyze global greedy and adversarial scheduling more generally. Our comparison concerns the full horizon profiles, forcing sequences and the common four-person orbit. We make no claim of bibliographic priority for these refinements.
